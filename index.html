Introduction · A Short History of Fossil Fuels

From Wood to Fossil Fuels: Dense, Reliable Energy

For most of history, people burned wood and relied on muscle, wind and water. Coal, then oil and natural gas, took over because they pack far more energy into every pound and can be stored, shipped and burned whenever they are needed.

  1. 1850
    Wood, muscle, water and wind

    In 1850, wood still supplied about 9 of every 10 units of energy Americans used. Mills sat beside rivers, and sailing ships waited on the wind.

  2. 1885
    Coal and steam take over

    Coal fed steam engines, railroads and factories, and passed wood as the leading U.S. energy source around 1885. The first U.S. coal-fired power plant opened in New York City in 1882.

  3. 1950
    Oil and the engine

    After the Spindletop gusher in Texas (1901), oil fueled the automobile age: U.S. cars grew from 8 million to more than 50 million between 1920 and 1950, the year petroleum passed coal.

  4. 2025
    Natural gas, and today

    Pipelines carried natural gas across the country. In 2025 fossil fuels supplied 82% of U.S. energy, and petroleum and natural gas were the two largest sources.

  5. Why they won dense and reliable

    Dense. A pound of gasoline holds about 5.4 kWh, three times seasoned firewood (1.85 kWh); house coal holds about twice as much. Less to haul, store and carry.

    Reliable. Fossil fuels are stored energy: piled, piped or pumped into a tank, then burned at any hour, in any season, far from where they were found. A waterwheel needs a river; a windmill needs wind.

How much wood would it take?

The firewood holding the same energy as the fuel for a car, a container ship and a jet airliner.

13.1 · What Are Fossil Fuels?

Three Fuels, One Ancient Origin

Nearly 80% of all energy powering modern lifestyles comes from fossil fuels, formed from ancient organisms buried under sediment for over 100 million years, subjected to extreme heat and pressure.

Coal
Solid fossil fuel from ancient tropical swamps. Burned primarily for electricity and heat. US holds 22% of global reserves.
Oil
Liquid hydrocarbon from ancient microscopic organisms. The world's most consumed fuel, primarily for transportation.
Natural Gas
Primarily methane. Fastest-growing fossil fuel; surpassed coal for US electricity generation in 2015.

13.1 · How Fossil Fuel Power Plants Work

From Fire to Wire: How Fossil Fuel Power Plants Work

Coal and natural gas plants are heat engines. They burn fuel to make heat, use the heat to spin a turbine, and turn the spin into electricity. Together they made about 58% of U.S. electricity in 2025.

  1. Burn
    Chemical energy becomes heat

    Coal or natural gas burns in a furnace. A coal plant burns about half a kilogram of coal (1.1 lb) for every kilowatt-hour it makes.

  2. Boil
    Heat becomes steam

    The fire heats purified water flowing through the boiler’s tubes into high-pressure steam at about 540 °C.

  3. Spin
    Steam spins a turbine and a generator

    Steam pushes rows of blades and spins a shaft 3,600 times a minute. A magnet on the shaft turns inside copper coils, driving current to the grid.

  4. Cool
    Most of the energy leaves as heat

    Spent steam is cooled back into water and reused. The leftover heat leaves through cooling towers and the stack: only about a third of coal’s energy becomes electricity.

  5. Coal vs. natural gas U.S. averages

    Fuel energy that becomes electricity32%45%combined cycle

    Most gas plants burn the fuel twice: in a jet-engine-like gas turbine, then again as its hot exhaust makes steam for a second turbine.

13.1 · How Fossil Fuel Power Plants Work

Where Does the Energy Go? Follow 100 Units of Fuel

Every step between the fuel and your lamp loses energy, mostly as heat. The losses multiply, so very little of the fuel’s energy ends up doing the job you wanted.

  1. Plant
    About two-thirds is lost at the plant

    A U.S. coal plant turns about 32 of every 100 units of fuel energy into electricity; a natural gas combined-cycle plant, about 45. The rest leaves as heat from the cooling tower and the stack.

    EIA heat rates, 2024 (see slide 8).
  2. Wires
    A little more on the wires

    About 5% of electricity is lost heating the power lines between the plant and your home.

    EIA, average for 2018–2022.
  3. Home
    Then the device decides

    A 60-watt incandescent bulb and a 9-watt LED give about the same light. The LED needs about 85% less electricity, and so about 85% less coal.

    FTC: about 800 lumens each; DOE: LEDs use at least 75% less energy.
  4. Nation
    The national picture

    In 2019, U.S. power plants used 38 quads of energy to deliver 14 quads of electricity: more than 60% was lost in conversion.

    EIA, Today in Energy (2020).
  5. Efficiencies multiply fuel needed for the same light
    IncandescentLED
    Coal plant (32%)100 units15 units
    Gas combined cycle (45%)71 units11 units

    100 × 0.32 × 0.95 = 30 units reach the outlet. Swapping the bulb saves more fuel than swapping the plant.

13.3 · What Drove the Rise of Fossil Fuels?

Economics and Government Policy

Scarcity → Innovation
Scarcity drives new technologies that expand proven reserves and lower costs — paradoxically increasing consumption (Jevons Paradox).
Government Action
England banned wood fuel and subsidized coal in the 1500s. Public infrastructure, tax incentives, and R&D funding shaped every transition.
Gradual Transitions
Coal took 300 years to surpass wood. Oil took 100+ years to surpass coal. Natural gas surpassed coal for US electricity only in 2015.

13.3 · Government Actions and the Rise of Fossil Fuels

Policy as a Driver of Energy Dominance

  • Governments have always shaped energy adoption, through regulation of competing sources, subsidies, public infrastructure, and research funding.
  • England banned wood fuel and subsidized coal in the 1500s, creating a guaranteed market. Tax structures and highway systems later enabled automobile and oil dominance.
  • Coal became the main heating fuel in England in the early 1600s, but it took 300 years for it to surpass wood as a dominant energy source.
  • Today, some governments subsidize fossil fuels, effectively lowering their market price and slowing the transition to alternatives.

13.4 · Coal — Extraction and Combustion

The Most Abundant and Most Polluting

  • The US, Russia, China, and Australia hold over two-thirds of global coal reserves.
  • Extraction: underground boring machines or surface blasting; mountaintop removal deposits waste in adjacent valleys, permanently altering landscapes.
  • Acid mine drainage destroys aquatic habitat; coal mines contribute 7% of US methane emissions through venting.
  • Coal emits significantly more CO₂ per unit of energy.

13.4 · Oil — Reserves, Extraction, and Impact

The World's Most Consumed Fuel

  • Middle East: 55% of global proven reserves | 54-year supply at current rates
  • Extracted by drilling and pumping; secondary extraction injects water; tertiary extraction uses steam.
  • Horizontal drilling and fracking: high-pressure water, sand, and chemicals fracture rock to release trapped oil.
  • Alberta tar sands require steam and direct heat, among the most energy-intensive extraction methods.
  • Burning oil accounts for 45% of US energy-related CO₂. The US has 72,000 miles of crude oil pipelines.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
13.4 · Oil — Reserves, Extraction, and Impact

Why the “number of years” changes:

Variables: use rate, technology, price per barrel.

  1. R/P
    Years left = reserves ÷ use

    In 2020 the world had about 1,732 billion barrels of proved reserves and used about 33 billion a year: about 53 years’ worth.

    BP Statistical Review (2021); Energy Institute via Our World in Data.
  2. Use
    Use rate

    Burn faster and the years shrink. World oil use passed 100 million barrels a day in 2023.

    Energy Institute, Statistical Review (2024).
  3. Tech
    Technology

    Wells recover only about 20 to 40% of the oil in a reservoir. Better drilling, fracking and enhanced recovery turn more of it into reserves.

    Muggeridge et al. (2014), Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A.
  4. Price
    Price per barrel

    Reserves count only oil worth producing at today’s prices. When prices rise, costly oil, such as Canada’s oil sands, becomes a reserve.

    EIA: proved reserves are recoverable “under existing economic and operating conditions.”

13.4 · Hydraulic Fracturing (Fracking)

Unlocking Unconventional Reserves

  1. Process
    How it works

    A well is drilled vertically then horizontally. High-pressure water, sand, and chemicals fracture the rock; sand holds fractures open while oil or gas flows out.

    Marcellus shale lies 5,000–9,000 ft down (USGS); new Permian laterals averaged 10,867 ft in 2025 (EIA).
  2. Why
    Why it matters

    Fracking has increased the amount of natural gas that can be profitably extracted from unconventional reserves.

    Fractured wells: under 7% of U.S. gas production in 2000, about two-thirds by 2015 (EIA).
  3. Water
    Groundwater risk

    Groundwater contamination risks from fracking fluids remain a major ongoing debate.

    EPA (2016): impacts “under some circumstances,” such as spills, faulty casing or cement, and poorly treated wastewater.
  4. Waste
    Water use and wastewater

    Requires large volumes of water; contaminated wastewater must be safely disposed of.

    Median 5.1 million gallons per horizontal gas well in 2014 (USGS); deep wastewater injection drove Oklahoma’s earthquake surge (USGS).

    water 88%sand 10%

    Additives are under 1% of the fluid, but a single well uses millions of gallons, so that is still thousands of gallons of chemicals.

13.5 · The Social Cost of Carbon

Pricing the True Cost of Emissions

Social Cost of Carbon: An economic measure of the damage caused by one additional ton of GHG emissions, including harm to property, agriculture, and human health. Currently estimated at a minimum of $51 per ton.

  1. Hidden
    An externality

    This cost is currently “externalized,” borne by society rather than by producers, making fossil fuels appear cheaper than they actually are.

    Rennert et al. (2022): about $185 per tonne, mostly heat-related deaths ($90) and lost crops ($84).
  2. Price
    Internalizing it

    Internalizing the social cost through carbon taxes or cap-and-trade makes the true price visible and changes incentives.

    At $190 a tonne, each gallon of gasoline carries about $1.69 of climate damage (8.9 kg of CO₂ per gallon, EPA).
  3. Clean
    Cheaper alternatives

    Wind and solar are already reducing costs.

  4. Who sets the number? dollars per tonne of CO₂

    U.S. interim estimate (2021)3%$51
    U.S. EPA (2023)2%$190

    In January 2025, Executive Order 14154 withdrew the federal estimates. Lower discount rates count future harm more and raise the number.

13.5 · Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS)

Preventing CO₂ from Reaching the Atmosphere

  • CCS captures CO₂ before it escapes into the atmosphere, then injects it underground for long-term storage — potentially enabling lower-carbon fossil fuel use.
  • Enhanced oil recovery (EOR): captured CO₂ is compressed and pumped underground to force additional oil from depleted wells.
  • CCS remains expensive and energy-intensive
  • Critics note that EOR enables continued fossil fuel extraction rather than reducing it.

13.6 · What Can I Do?

Beyond Fossil Fuels

  • Choose transportation with lower fossil fuel use, higher mpg vehicles, public transit, or EVs.
  • Select products with smaller fossil fuel footprints across their production and supply chains.
  • Find out what commitments your institution or employer has made to reducing fossil fuel use.
  • Stay informed: carbon taxes, cap-and-trade, and CCS debates affect everyone's energy future.
